I'm Vikas — a WordPress and Elementor developer in Indore.

Eight years, Eighteen documented projects, nine countries. Most of it for organisations outside India: a Swiss implant manufacturer, a regulated investment firm in Dubai, a century-old American peace organisation, a whiskey producer in Limerick, a beverage brand in Austria.

Almost none of it glamorous. All of it work where being wrong costs the client money — a checkout that fails, a compliance page that says the wrong thing, a site nobody internally can update.

That's the work I'm good at, and it's a narrower thing than "WordPress developer" suggests. I'm not the person to invent your brand or write your copy. I'm the person who takes a requirement that sounds simple, finds the four things in it that aren't, and tells you about them before you've committed a budget.

How I work

Three things
I hold to.

Not values on a wall. These are the three rules that decide how a project actually runs, and the ones a client will notice within the first week.

Say the constraint first

If the deadline can't be met properly, if the requirement will cause a problem in year two, if the budget doesn't cover what's been described — you hear it on the first call. Not at handover, and not in an invoice.

This loses me some work. It's the reason the projects I do take tend to finish.

The build follows the maintainer

Elementor or a custom theme isn't a matter of taste — it depends entirely on who edits the site after I'm gone. If your marketing person needs to rearrange a page, that decides the build. If nobody will ever touch the layout, that decides it differently.

Everything in writing

Scope with a fixed price and dated milestones. A written update every Friday whether or not there's good news. Handover documentation and a recorded walkthrough. If it wasn't written down, it wasn't agreed.

The obvious concern

Hiring one person.

You are considering paying a stranger on another continent to build something your business depends on. That deserves a straight answer rather than reassurance.

Here are the four questions people are usually too polite to ask.

What if you disappear?

It's the right question and the honest answer isn't "I won't." The answer is that every project is built so someone else could pick it up. Standard WordPress structure, no proprietary framework, documented custom code, credentials in your name, hosting on your account.

If I vanished tomorrow you'd hand another developer the documentation and they'd be productive in a day. That's not a promise about me; it's a property of how the work is built.

Can one person handle our project?

Sometimes no. I take on two or three projects at a time, which means occasionally the honest answer is that your timeline needs a team and I'm not it. I'd rather say that than stretch and deliver late.

Where one person is an advantage: nothing is lost in translation between the person you brief and the person who writes the code, because they're the same person.

Are you actually available, or juggling twelve clients?

Two or three at once, deliberately. Current availability is stated on the homepage and kept accurate — if it says Q3, that's real. Response time is one business day, usually the same day.

What about the time difference?

There's roughly four hours of daily overlap with the UK and Europe, and early-morning overlap with the US East Coast. Most of the work happens asynchronously anyway, with a written update every Friday. Calls get scheduled in your hours, not mine.

Eighteen projects across nine countries have run this way. It's a solved problem, not a risk.
