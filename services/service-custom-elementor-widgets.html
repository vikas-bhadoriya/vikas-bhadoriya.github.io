The addon problem

What a bundle
actually costs.

Addon packs aren't badly made. They're just built to serve everyone, which means carrying weight your site will never use — and that weight is on every page load, forever.

Assets on every page

Most bundles enqueue their stylesheet and script globally because they can't know which widget you'll use where. Two hundred kilobytes to render one element on one page.

Three bundles, one requirement

Each was installed for a single widget nobody else had. Now three packs load together, occasionally conflict, and every update is a small risk to a site that was working.

It still isn't quite right

The widget does eighty per cent of what the design needs. The last twenty is patched with custom CSS that breaks the next time the addon updates its markup.

What you get

Six things,
every widget.

Built against Elementor's own widget API, so it behaves like a native control rather than something bolted alongside the editor.

  • A native editor experience

    Appears in the widget panel with your icon and category. Full Content, Style and Advanced tabs, live preview while editing, and responsive controls per breakpoint. Your team won't know it wasn't built in.

  • Conditional asset loading

    The widget's CSS and JavaScript load only on pages where the widget is actually placed. This is the whole point, and it's the thing addon bundles structurally cannot do.

  • Controls that match your design system

    Colour, typography and spacing pickers wired to your global settings rather than hard-coded values, so a brand change updates the widget along with everything else.

  • Dynamic content support

    Where useful, the widget pulls from ACF fields, custom post types or WooCommerce data rather than requiring content to be re-typed into the widget itself.

  • Accessible and semantic output

    Proper markup, keyboard operability, ARIA only where genuinely needed, visible focus. Interactive widgets — accordions, tabs, sliders — are where accessibility most often gets skipped.

  • Delivered as a plugin, owned by you

    Packaged as a standalone plugin so it survives theme changes, documented, in Git. No licence, no per-site fee, and reusable across as many of your sites as you like.

Common requests

What people
usually need.

Roughly in order of how often they come up. If yours isn't here it's probably still straightforward — most widgets are a day or two.

  • A filtered post or product loop — pulling custom post types with taxonomy filters, which the native loop widget doesn't quite reach.
  • A pricing or comparison table driven by fields rather than typed into the page and re-typed on every edit.
  • An interactive element from the design — a tabbed panel, an accordion, a step process — built to spec rather than approximated.
  • A third-party data display — reviews, availability, stock, an external API's output rendered inside the editor.
  • A form with logic the standard form widget can't express — conditional fields, calculations, a multi-step flow.
  • A replacement for one addon bundle you installed for a single widget and would like to remove.

Questions

Asked before
commissioning.

Mostly about whether it's worth paying for something an addon pack appears to give away.

Why not just use an addon pack?

If you need eight of its ninety widgets, do — that's what bundles are for and I'd say so.

The maths changes when you need one. A $59-a-year bundle costs about 200KB on every page load forever, plus an annual renewal and an update risk. A $250 one-off widget loads on the two pages that use it and never renews. It usually pays back inside two years and improves performance immediately.

Will it break when Elementor updates?

It shouldn't. Widgets are built against Elementor's documented widget API, which is stable and versioned — that's what it exists for.

What breaks on update is CSS hacks targeting Elementor's internal class names, which is exactly the patching a custom widget removes the need for.

Can our team use it without training?

Yes. It appears in the widget panel like any other, drags onto the page like any other, and its settings sit in the same Content, Style and Advanced tabs your team already uses. Controls are labelled in plain language rather than developer terms.

Can we use it on our other sites?

Yes. It's delivered as a standalone plugin, you own the code, and there's no licensing or per-site fee. Agencies commonly commission a widget once and deploy it across several client sites — that's an expected use.

Can you replace an addon pack we already have?

Usually. Tell me which widgets from it you actually use — often it's two or three — and I'll quote replacing those. Removing the bundle afterwards typically takes a noticeable amount off every page.

Existing pages need the old widgets swapped for the new ones, which is included in the quote.

Does this work with Elementor Free or does it need Pro?

Both. The widget API is available in the free version, so a custom widget works either way. If your requirement is otherwise only met by a Pro feature, I'll tell you whether upgrading or building is the better value.
