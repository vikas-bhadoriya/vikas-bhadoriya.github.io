Why people call

Three versions
of the same call.

Almost every rescue starts one of these three ways. What they share is that nobody can tell you the real state of the thing you've already paid for — and until someone can, every quote you get is guesswork.

They vanished mid-build

Payments made, work part-delivered, messages unanswered for weeks. You don't know how much is done, whether what exists is any good, or whether you can legally use it.

It's live but breaking

The site works until it doesn't. Something goes wrong every few weeks, nobody knows why, and each fix seems to cause the next problem. Nobody has ever looked at the whole picture.

You've inherited it

New role, new agency, or the person who built it left. There's no documentation, the plugin list is a mystery, and you've been asked to make changes to something nobody understands.

What gets checked

Eight areas,
every audit.

The same checklist regardless of what you think the problem is — because the reported symptom and the actual cause are usually in different places.

  • What you actually own

    Domain registrar, hosting account, WordPress admin, repository, licences for premium themes and plugins. Who each is registered to, and what's missing. This is first because it's the thing that most often turns out to be wrong.

  • Security state

    Known vulnerabilities in current versions, malware and injected-code scan, file permissions, user accounts that shouldn't exist, SSL configuration, and whether anything has already been compromised.

  • Code quality and custom work

    What's custom versus off the shelf, whether custom code was written into the theme in a way updates will destroy, and whether anyone else could reasonably take it over.

  • Performance, with causes

    Core Web Vitals measured on real mobile hardware, then the actual reasons — render-blocking assets, unoptimised images, database queries, server response time. Not a score, a cause list.

  • Plugin and theme inventory

    Everything installed, its update status, whether it's still maintained, whether two plugins are doing the same job, and which ones are quietly costing you a second of load time.

  • Hosting and infrastructure

    Whether the plan matches the traffic, PHP and database versions, backup arrangement and — the part people assume — whether a restore has ever actually been tested.

  • Completion state

    For abandoned builds: what's finished, what's half-built, what was never started. Measured against your original brief where you have one.

  • Editability and handover risk

    Whether your team can maintain it without a developer, what's undocumented, and what would break if a specific person disappeared tomorrow.

Scope

What an audit
isn't.

This is a diagnosis, not a treatment. Being clear about that is what keeps the price where it is.

  • No fixes are made. Nothing on your site is touched. If something critical is found, you hear immediately rather than at day five.
  • Not a legal service. I can tell you what you don't control; recovering a domain from an uncooperative party is a lawyer's job.
  • Not a design review. Whether the site looks good isn't a question I'm answering here.
  • Not an SEO audit. Technical issues that affect search get flagged, but content and link strategy are outside scope.
  • Not a guarantee everything is findable. Without the original developer's cooperation some history is unrecoverable. I'll say what I couldn't determine rather than guess.

Afterwards

Three ways
this ends.

Roughly a third of audits end with me doing nothing further, and that's a legitimate outcome rather than a failure. The report is the product.

It's fine, actually

Sometimes the site is in better shape than feared and needs two days of work, not a rebuild. You get told that, and you've spent $400 to avoid spending $8,000 you didn't need to.

Repair, not replace

Most common outcome. A costed fix list in priority order, which you can hand to me, to your existing team, or to anyone else. The report is written to be portable.

Rebuild is cheaper

Occasionally what exists costs more to repair than to replace. When that's true you get told plainly, with both numbers side by side rather than just the one that suits me.

Questions

What people ask
before booking.

Usually asked in a slightly panicked email. Answered here so you can decide at 11pm without waiting for a reply.

I don't have admin access. Can you still audit it?

Partly. A surprising amount can be assessed from the outside — performance, security headers, the technology in use, what's publicly exposed. But code quality, plugin state and ownership can't be.

Tell me what access you do have and I'll say honestly what's possible before you pay. If it's too little to be useful, I'll say that instead of taking the fee.

What if the previous developer refuses to hand things over?

The audit tells you exactly what they're holding and what it's worth — which is the information you need before deciding whether to negotiate, escalate or walk away and rebuild.

In practice a lot of "held hostage" situations turn out to be recoverable directly through the registrar or host, because the account is in your company's name even when nobody realised it. That's checked first.

Will you tell me my site needs a rebuild so you can sell one?

It's the right thing to be suspicious of. Two structural reasons it doesn't happen: the fee is the same whatever I find, and it's credited against repair work too — so recommending repair costs me nothing.

The report gives both routes with both numbers. If repair is cheaper, that's what it says, and you're free to have someone else do it.

Is my site's data safe during the audit?

Nothing is modified — it's read-only throughout. I work from a staging copy where one exists. Credentials go in a password manager and are deleted at the end unless you ask me to keep them. NDA signed before you send anything, if you'd prefer.

My site is down right now. Is this the right thing to buy?

No — get it back up first. Email me with "urgent" in the subject and I'll tell you whether it's something you can fix in ten minutes or something I should look at. Emergency work is billed hourly.

The audit is for understanding a site's overall state, not for firefighting.

Can I use the report to get quotes from other developers?

Yes, and it's written for exactly that. A vague brief gets you wildly varying quotes; a specific findings list gets you comparable ones. Several audits have ended with the client hiring someone local, which is a fine result.

We're an agency who inherited a client's site. Does this work for us?

Yes, and it's a common use. White-label as standard — the report can be issued unbranded for you to pass on as your own assessment, with no contact between me and your client. Terms here.

What if you find something serious on day one?

You hear immediately. Active malware, an exposed database or a site already compromised doesn't wait until the report is finished. I'll tell you what needs doing that day, whether or not I'm the one who does it.
