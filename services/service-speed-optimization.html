Why the usual advice fails

Three things
that don't work.

Almost every slow site I'm sent has already had these tried. They're not wrong exactly — they're just not the reason your particular site is slow, and doing them harder won't change that.

Installing another cache

Caching helps a page that's expensive to build. It does nothing for a page that's cheap to build and heavy to download — and stacking two caching plugins usually makes things worse, not faster.

Chasing the desktop score

A green desktop score on a fibre connection tells you almost nothing. Google ranks on mobile field data from real visitors on real networks, which is a different measurement entirely.

Deleting plugins at random

Plugin count isn't the metric — impact is. One badly written plugin can cost more than fifteen well-written ones, and removing the wrong fifteen just breaks the site.

Where the time goes

Seven causes,
in the order
they usually matter.

This is roughly the order I find them in. The first three account for most of the problem on most sites — and none of them are solved by a plugin.

  • Images doing far too much work

    A 3000px photograph displayed at 600px, served as JPEG, with no dimensions set so the layout jumps while it loads. Usually the single largest win, and usually the least interesting one.

  • Render-blocking CSS and JavaScript

    The browser can't paint anything until it has finished downloading and parsing everything in the head. Page builders and addon packs are the common culprits — often loading assets for widgets the page doesn't use.

  • Third-party scripts

    Chat widgets, analytics, pixels, review embeds, font services. Each one is somebody else's server deciding how fast your site is. Frequently 40% of total load, and almost always loadable later.

  • Database queries

    Unindexed queries, a post-meta table that's grown to hundreds of thousands of rows, an autoloaded options table full of data from plugins deleted two years ago. Slow before a single byte is sent.

  • Server and PHP configuration

    An outdated PHP version, no object cache, shared hosting with a neighbour consuming the CPU. Sometimes the honest answer is that the hosting is the problem and $15 a month fixes it.

  • Fonts

    Four weights loaded from a third-party service when two self-hosted ones would do, with no fallback strategy — so text is invisible while they download.

  • Layout instability

    Not speed exactly, but Google measures it and users hate it. Elements shifting as images, ads and embeds arrive. Almost always fixable with dimensions and reserved space rather than anything clever.

How it runs

Diagnose first,
then fix.

Two separate stages, deliberately. You can stop after the diagnosis and hand the findings to anyone — several clients have, and that's fine.

STAGE 01

Measure properly

Lab tests on throttled mobile hardware plus your real field data from Search Console, so we're fixing what visitors experience rather than what a desktop test says.

STAGE 02

Find the causes

Waterfall analysis, query profiling, asset audit, third-party inventory. Not "your site is slow" — a ranked list of what is taking the time.

STAGE 03

Written report

Each cause with its measured cost in milliseconds, the fix, the effort, and the expected gain. You decide what's worth doing.

STAGE 04

Fix on staging

Every change made and verified on a staging copy first. Nothing touches your live site until it's proven not to break anything.

STAGE 05

Prove it

Before and after on the same tests, plus a note on when field data will catch up — because Core Web Vitals lag by 28 days.

Scope

What I
won't do.

Mostly things that improve a score without improving the site. A number is not the point; a site that feels fast to a real person is.

  • Chase a score with tricks. Deferring everything until after the test fires makes the number look good and the site feel worse.
  • Strip functionality to hit a target. If removing your booking form would help, you'll hear it as a trade-off — not as a decision I make quietly.
  • Promise a specific number before diagnosis. Anyone guaranteeing "90+" without seeing the site is guessing, and you should treat that as the warning it is.
  • Blame the host by default. Sometimes it is the host. Usually it isn't, and switching costs you a migration that changes nothing.
  • Rebuild the site. That's a different service. If a rebuild is genuinely cheaper than the fix list, the report says so with both numbers.

Expectations

What you can
reasonably expect.

No guarantees of a specific score, because anyone offering one before seeing your site is selling rather than diagnosing. What I can describe is the shape of a typical outcome.

Most sites: substantial gain

A site that has never had performance work usually has three or four large, unglamorous problems. Fixing those moves it from noticeably slow to comfortably fast without touching the design.

Some sites: a ceiling

If the business needs a chat widget, a review embed and three tracking pixels, there's a floor below which load time won't go. I'll tell you where that floor is rather than pretending otherwise.

Field data lags

Core Web Vitals in Search Console use a 28-day rolling window. Lab tests improve immediately; the data Google ranks on takes about a month to catch up. Plan launches accordingly.

Questions

Asked before
every project.

If yours isn't here, send the URL — I'll usually be able to say something useful about it before you commit to anything.

Can you guarantee a 90+ PageSpeed score?

No, and I'd be careful with anyone who does. The score depends on things outside my control — third-party scripts your business needs, hosting you may not want to change, functionality that costs what it costs.

What I'll give you after the diagnosis is a realistic target for your specific site, and I'll tell you before any fix work starts if that target is lower than you were hoping.

My site is on Elementor. Is that the problem?

Usually it's how it was built rather than the tool itself. Containers nested five deep, six addon packs loaded to render two widgets, assets loading on every page regardless of use — those are build decisions, and most of them are fixable without rebuilding.

Occasionally the build is so tangled that rebuilding specific templates is cheaper than untangling them. If that's the case the report says so, with both costs. How I build in Elementor.

Will this improve my Google rankings?

Core Web Vitals are a confirmed ranking signal, but a modest one — speed alone won't outrank better content. Where it reliably matters is when you're roughly level with competitors on everything else, and on mobile conversion, where slow pages measurably lose sales.

Treat ranking improvement as a possible side effect and conversion improvement as the actual reason.

Why pay for a diagnosis when PageSpeed Insights is free?

PageSpeed tells you what's wrong, not why, and its recommendations are generic. "Reduce unused JavaScript" is accurate and useless — the question is which script, loaded by what, whether it's needed, and what happens if it's deferred.

The report answers those, with measured costs per issue and a ranked order. It's the difference between a symptom list and a diagnosis.

Will you break anything?

Every change is made and tested on a staging copy first, then verified on the live site afterwards. Performance work does carry real risk — aggressive deferral breaks sliders, over-eager minification breaks forms — which is exactly why nothing goes straight to production.

Do I need to change hosting?

Sometimes, and I'll say so plainly when server response time is genuinely the bottleneck. More often it's third or fourth on the list and switching would be an expensive distraction. The report tells you where hosting actually ranks among your problems.

How long until I see results in Search Console?

Lab tests improve the day the work ships. Search Console uses a 28-day rolling average of real visitor data, so expect roughly a month before the field metrics reflect the change, and slightly longer for a low-traffic site.

Will it stay fast?

Not automatically. Performance regresses — someone adds a plugin, uploads a 4MB hero image, or a marketing team installs a tracking script. Every fix includes a short written note on what to avoid, and monthly monitoring is available if you'd rather not think about it.
