The honest version

When custom
is wrong.

This is the most oversold service in WordPress. A custom theme is the right answer less often than developers who sell them like to admit, so here's when it isn't.

Your team edits layouts

If marketing needs to rearrange a page next quarter without a developer, you want a page builder built well — not a custom theme that requires a ticket for every change. Elementor, built properly.

The budget is under $2,500

Below that I can't do the content modelling and testing properly, and a rushed custom theme is worse than a good off-the-shelf one. A well-built Elementor site is the better use of the money.

Nothing about the site is unusual

A standard business site with standard pages doesn't need bespoke code. Custom earns its cost when the content structure or the performance requirement is genuinely particular.

When it's right

Seven things
a custom theme
gives you.

Where the case does hold, these are the reasons — and most of them compound over years rather than showing up on launch day.

  • A content model that matches your business

    Custom post types and fields shaped around what you actually publish — projects, products, case studies, locations — rather than forcing everything into posts and pages and hoping categories hold it together.

  • Only the code your site runs

    No demo importers, no bundled sliders, no options panel with four hundred settings. Typically a fraction of the CSS and JavaScript of a commercial theme, because there's nothing in it you don't use.

  • Performance as a starting point

    Assets loaded per template, images handled properly, no render-blocking third-party libraries. Green Core Web Vitals is far easier when nothing unnecessary is in the build to begin with.

  • An editing experience designed for your team

    Either custom Gutenberg blocks or ACF field groups, so the edit screen shows the fields your content needs and nothing else. Non-technical staff publish confidently because there's nothing to get wrong.

  • No third-party dependency

    No theme author who might abandon it, raise the licence, or ship an update that changes your layout. The code stops changing unless you change it.

  • Accessibility built into the markup

    Semantic structure, keyboard operability, proper heading order and labelled controls — decided at template level, which is far cheaper than retrofitting into a purchased theme's output.

  • Documented for whoever comes next

    Template structure, hooks, field groups and build process, written down. WordPress coding standards throughout, so another developer recognises the shape immediately.

How it runs

Five stages.

Content modelling comes before templates, because the structure decides everything after it. Getting that order wrong is the most expensive mistake in a theme build.

STAGE 01

Content model

What content types exist, what fields each needs, how they relate. Signed off before any template is written.

STAGE 02

Foundation

Type scale, colour, spacing and shared components built first, so the last template is as consistent as the first.

STAGE 03

Templates

Built in agreed order on a staging link you can watch, with real content rather than placeholder text.

STAGE 04

Editing layer

Blocks or field groups, then your team tries publishing something real before launch — the point at which problems are cheap.

STAGE 05

Handover

Performance and accessibility tested, repository, documentation, recorded walkthrough. 30 days of fixes.

Scope

What's not
included.

Available, quoted separately rather than assumed.

  • Design. I need a Figma, XD or PSD file. I build what a designer specifies; I don't originate visual direction.
  • Copywriting. Bring final copy or a plan for replacing placeholders.
  • Content migration. Moving three hundred posts into the new structure is separate work.
  • WooCommerce templating. Store templates are a meaningful addition — see WooCommerce.
  • A theme for sale. Building for public distribution means supporting unknown configurations. Different job, different cost.

Questions

Asked before
committing.

A custom theme is a bigger commitment than buying one, and the concerns are usually about what happens in year three.

Custom theme or Elementor?

It depends entirely on who maintains the site. If your team needs to rearrange layouts themselves, Elementor built carefully is the right answer and the performance difference is small. If nobody will ever touch the layout, a custom theme is leaner and cheaper to run.

The tool follows the maintainer. I'll ask who edits the site before recommending either. How I build in Elementor.

Should it be a block theme?

Block themes and full site editing are where WordPress is going, and for a content-led site with a team already comfortable in the block editor they're a good choice.

For a site with complex custom fields and relationships, a classic theme with ACF is still more practical today. I'll say which fits rather than defaulting to whichever is fashionable.

What happens when WordPress releases a major update?

Very little, because the theme uses documented template functions and hooks. WordPress takes backwards compatibility seriously, and properly built themes routinely survive a decade of updates.

What breaks on update is code depending on undocumented internals or a specific plugin's output. Both are avoided.

Can our team still edit pages?

Yes — that's a build requirement, not a trade-off. Either custom Gutenberg blocks or ACF field groups, so the edit screen shows exactly the fields the content needs.

What they can't do is restructure a template's layout. That's the deliberate difference from a page builder, and whether it's a benefit or a limitation is exactly the question in the first FAQ.

Why not buy a $60 theme and customise it?

For a straightforward site, genuinely do that — I'd rather tell you than take the money. It's the fastest route to a decent site on a small budget.

The cost surfaces later: heavy code you can't remove, an update that changes your layout, and customisations that fight the theme's own assumptions. That's when the total exceeds a custom build.

Can you rebuild our existing site as a custom theme?

Yes, and content usually transfers cleanly since it lives in the database rather than the theme. The work is mapping existing content into a better structure — often the most valuable part, because the original structure is usually the real problem.
