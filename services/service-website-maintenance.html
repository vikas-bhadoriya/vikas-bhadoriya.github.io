What goes wrong

Three failures,
all preventable.

Nothing here is exotic. These are the three things that actually take sites down, and all three are cheaper to prevent than to recover from.

The update that broke everything

A plugin auto-updated at 3am and took the checkout with it. Nobody noticed until a customer emailed. Updates tested on staging first turn this from an outage into a non-event.

The backup that wasn't

Backups were running for two years. Nobody ever tried restoring one. When it was needed, it was incomplete — or on the same server that just failed.

The vulnerability nobody saw

A plugin was abandoned by its author eighteen months ago and quietly became a known entry point. It still works perfectly, which is precisely why nobody looked at it.

What's covered

Seven things,
every plan.

The differences between plans are hours, response time and reporting depth. The fundamentals below are on every plan including the cheapest.

  • Updates tested before they ship

    Core, theme and plugin updates applied to a staging copy first and checked against the pages that matter — checkout, forms, anything custom. Only then to production. Auto-updates are switched off, because that's the entire problem.

  • Off-server backups, restore-tested

    Daily backups stored away from the web server, retained for 30 days. A restore is performed and verified quarterly, because a backup nobody has restored is a hope rather than a plan.

  • Uptime and error monitoring

    Checked every five minutes from multiple locations, with PHP error and 404 monitoring. If it goes down at 2am I know at 2am — most of the time it's back before you've noticed.

  • Security hardening and scanning

    Malware scanning, file integrity checks, login protection, and a review of any plugin whose author has stopped maintaining it. Abandoned plugins get flagged and replaced before they become the story.

  • Performance kept honest

    Core Web Vitals checked monthly against real field data. Performance regresses quietly — someone uploads a 4MB image or marketing adds a tracking script — and you get told when it does.

  • Development hours included

    Real hours for real changes, not just "support". Content updates, a new section, fixing something that annoys you. Unused hours roll over one month, so a quiet month isn't wasted.

  • A written monthly report

    What was updated, what was found, how the site is performing, and hours used. Short enough to actually read, and specific enough to prove the money is doing something.

Scope

What a plan
isn't.

Care plans go wrong when "maintenance" quietly means "unlimited free development". Naming the edge keeps the price honest.

  • Not unlimited development. Hours are stated per plan. Larger work is quoted separately, at the reduced rate.
  • Not a redesign. Keeping the site working is not the same as changing what it is.
  • Not hosting. You keep your own account, in your name. I'll recommend one and configure it, but I won't resell it to you at a markup.
  • Not content production. I'll publish what you send; writing it is a different job.
  • Not marketing or SEO. Technical health is covered; campaigns and content strategy aren't.
  • Not a guarantee of zero downtime. Hosts fail and networks break. What's promised is fast detection, tested backups and someone responding — not that nothing will ever go wrong.

Questions

Asked before
signing up.

If yours isn't here, ask — a care plan is a monthly commitment and you should be sure before starting one.

Can you maintain a site you didn't build?

Yes, and about half of them are. There's a short review first to establish what state it's in — occasionally a site needs a few hours of remedial work before a plan makes sense, and you'd hear that before committing rather than discovering it in month three.

If it's in genuinely poor shape, a rescue audit is the better starting point.

Isn't this what my host already does?

Hosts keep the server running and usually take backups. That's genuinely valuable and it isn't the same thing.

They won't test a plugin update against your checkout, notice that a plugin was abandoned by its author, tell you why the site got slower last month, or make the changes you need. Managed hosting plus no maintenance is still a site nobody is looking after.

Why not just use a plugin to auto-update everything?

Because auto-updates are how most sites break. The update itself is usually fine; the interaction between an updated plugin and your specific setup is what fails, and it fails while nobody is watching.

Testing on staging first costs twenty minutes and prevents the 3am outage. That's most of what you're paying for.

What counts as an emergency?

The site is down, the checkout is broken, or it's been compromised. Those get the emergency response time on your plan.

"The heading is the wrong colour" is a normal request and gets normal turnaround, however urgent it feels on the day.

What happens to the included hours if I don't use them?

They roll over one month, so a quiet January can fund a busy February. They don't accumulate indefinitely — the plan is reserved capacity rather than a savings account, and I'd rather price it honestly than pretend otherwise.

Can I cancel?

Any month, no fee, no notice period. Everything lives on your hosting under your accounts, so cancelling means the monitoring stops — nothing is withheld and nothing needs migrating back.

What if the site gets hacked while on a plan?

Cleanup and restoration are included. That's the point of tested backups — recovery is a known procedure rather than an emergency negotiation.

If the compromise came through something I flagged and you chose not to act on, that's still fixed; you'd just have had the warning in writing beforehand.

Do you offer this white-label for agencies?

Yes. Reports issued unbranded for you to pass on, no contact with your client, invoiced to you. Several agencies resell maintenance this way at their own margin. Terms here.
