Why people move

Four reasons,
one process.

The reason changes what's checked afterwards, but the method is the same: build the new environment fully, verify it against the old one, then switch — rather than moving and hoping.

The host is the problem

Slow server response, frequent downtime, or support that takes three days to answer. Sometimes the honest fix for a slow site really is a better host — and sometimes it isn't, which you'd hear first.

You're taking control back

The site sits on an agency's or a previous developer's account. You want it in your name, on your billing, with credentials you actually hold.

Something changed

New domain, rebrand, merging two sites, splitting one, or moving from a subdomain to a proper domain. All of these are migrations plus a redirect strategy.

What's covered

Seven things
that get missed.

Copying files and a database is the easy part, and it's where most cheap migrations stop. These are the things that cause the problems afterwards.

  • Email records, checked before the switch

    The classic disaster. Move the domain's DNS without carrying MX and SPF records across and email stops arriving — often silently, and often for days. Records are documented before anything changes and verified after.

  • Every URL still resolves

    Full URL inventory before the move, tested after. Where structure changes, 301 redirects mapped one by one rather than a blanket rule that sends everything to the homepage and destroys your rankings.

  • Database search-replace done correctly

    Domain references live inside serialised data. A plain find-and-replace corrupts it and breaks widgets and settings in ways that surface weeks later. Done with a tool that understands serialisation.

  • SSL live before the cutover

    Certificate issued and tested on the new server first, so there's no window where visitors see a security warning. Mixed-content references cleaned up at the same time.

  • DNS with the TTL lowered in advance

    TTL reduced 24–48 hours ahead so propagation is minutes rather than a day. Both servers stay live during the change, so whichever one a visitor reaches, the site works.

  • Everything that isn't the website

    Cron jobs, scheduled tasks, API integrations, payment gateway callbacks, form handlers, third-party webhooks. These break silently and nobody notices until an order doesn't arrive.

  • A tested rollback

    The old environment stays intact and reachable for fourteen days. If something surfaces on day three, reverting takes minutes rather than being an emergency.

How it runs

Five stages,

The cutover itself takes minutes. Everything before it is preparation, which is exactly why it takes minutes.

Monitoring for 48 hours, redirects and email confirmed working, old environment kept ready for 14 days.

Scope

What's not
included.

A migration moves what exists. Improving it is separate work, quoted separately.

  • Fixing what was already broken. A slow or buggy site is a slow or buggy site on the new host too. See speed optimisation.
  • Hosting costs. The account is yours, in your name, billed directly. I don't resell hosting at a markup.
  • Design or content changes. A migration is a move, not a redesign.
  • Recovering access from an uncooperative party. If someone won't hand over the domain, that's a legal matter — though a rescue audit establishes exactly what they're holding.
  • Platform conversion. Moving from Shopify or Squarespace to WordPress is a rebuild, not a migration.

Questions

Asked before
every move.

Migration anxiety is mostly about the unknown. These are the four things people are actually worried about.

Will the site really be down for zero minutes?

Yes, in practice. The new server is live and serving the full site before DNS changes, and the old one stays running throughout — so whichever address a visitor's DNS resolves to during propagation, they get a working site.

The exception is a store, where I'll pause order processing for a few minutes at the switch so no order lands on a server that's about to stop being used. That's scheduled around your quietest hour.

Will our email keep working?

Yes, and it's the first thing checked rather than an afterthought. MX, SPF, DKIM and DMARC records are documented before any change and verified after, with a test message sent both directions.

This is the most common thing to go wrong in a cheap migration, usually because someone changed nameservers without carrying the mail records across.

Will we lose search rankings?

Same domain, same URLs: no, there's nothing for Google to re-learn. A faster server sometimes helps slightly.

New domain or changed URLs: expect a temporary dip while Google processes the redirects, typically a few weeks. Per-page 301s and a Search Console change-of-address notification are what keep it temporary rather than permanent.

What if something breaks after the move?

The old environment stays intact and reachable for fourteen days, so reverting is a DNS change rather than a restore. I monitor for 48 hours afterwards, and anything caused by the migration is fixed at no charge.

Which host should we move to?

Depends on the site — traffic, whether it's a store, whether anyone technical will ever log into the server. I'll recommend two or three options with honest trade-offs and no affiliate arrangement, because I don't have any.

You open and own the account. I configure it.

Can you move a site you don't have full access to?

Often, yes — file and database access is usually enough even without a control panel login. What can't be worked around is domain control, since somebody has to change the DNS.

Tell me what access you have and I'll say plainly whether it's workable before you pay anything.
