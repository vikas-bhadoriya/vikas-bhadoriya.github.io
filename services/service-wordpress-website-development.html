What goes wrong

Three ways
a build disappoints.

Rarely on launch day. The disappointment arrives three to twelve months later, and all three of these are decisions made at the start rather than accidents at the end.

Every change needs a developer

The site looks right and is unmaintainable. Updating a paragraph means an email, a quote and three days. Within a year the content is stale because changing it is too much trouble.

It got slow immediately

Fast on launch day with placeholder content. Then real images went in, marketing added two tracking scripts, and now it takes five seconds on a phone.

It can't grow

Built for the pages that existed then. Adding a case study section or a second language means rebuilding, because the content structure was never designed to hold anything new.

What's included

Seven things,
every build.

Not upsells. These are in the price on every project, because a site missing any of them isn't finished — it just looks finished.

  • A content structure built to grow

    Custom post types and fields designed around what you publish and what you'll publish next. Adding a new section later becomes an afternoon rather than a rebuild.

  • An editing layer matched to your team

    Elementor if people need to move things themselves, a locked structure with open content if they don't. Decided by who maintains the site, not by what I prefer building.

  • Performance from the start

    Green Core Web Vitals on mobile is the target, tested on real hardware before handover — not a separate optimisation project sold to you afterwards.

  • Technical SEO done properly

    Clean URL structure, correct heading hierarchy, meta control, schema markup, XML sitemap, robots handling and redirects from any old URLs. The foundation, not the campaign.

  • Accessibility as a baseline

    Semantic markup, keyboard operability, visible focus states, labelled forms, contrast checked. If the design fails contrast you hear before the build, not after.

  • Security and backups configured

    Hardened install, no default admin account, SSL, off-server backups running and — the part usually skipped — a restore actually tested before handover.

  • Handover that transfers real control

    All credentials in your name, documentation, and a recorded walkthrough of the things your team will actually do. You could hire anyone else the next day.

Questions

Asked before
every build.

If yours isn't here, twenty minutes on a call usually settles it faster than email.

Why WordPress rather than Webflow, Squarespace or a framework?

Ownership and exit. WordPress runs on any host, is maintainable by tens of thousands of developers, and nobody can raise your subscription or change your feature set unilaterally.

If you want a simple brochure site and don't mind the platform owning it, Squarespace is genuinely fine and cheaper — I'd say so rather than talk you out of it. WordPress earns its place when the content structure is particular or you want the option to leave.

Do you use a page builder or write it from scratch?

Whichever suits the people maintaining it. Elementor when your team needs to rearrange layouts — built lean, with custom PHP widgets rather than addon packs. A custom theme when nobody will touch the layout.

I'll ask who edits the site before recommending either, because that answer decides it.

What do you need from us?

A design file if you have one, final copy or a plan for it, image assets, and one person empowered to give feedback and make decisions. That last one matters more than the rest — projects stall on approval by committee far more often than on anything technical.

How much of our time will this take?

A kickoff call, roughly an hour a week reviewing staging, and content delivered on the dates in the scope. The written scope names who owes what and when, because waiting on material is the single biggest cause of overrun.

Will we lose search rankings when we relaunch?

Not if URLs are preserved or properly redirected, which is part of every rebuild. I map old URLs to new ones individually rather than using a blanket rule, keep the heading structure sound, and verify indexing after launch.

Expect a small fluctuation for a week or two while Google re-crawls. A sustained drop means something was missed, and that's on me to fix.

What happens after launch?

Bugs in what I built are fixed at no charge for 30 days, and beyond that if they're genuinely defects. Documentation and a recorded walkthrough mean your team is self-sufficient.

If you'd rather not think about updates, backups and monitoring, a care plan covers it. It's optional, and nothing is withheld if you decline.
